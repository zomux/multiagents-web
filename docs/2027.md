## Overview

Modern agent deployments rarely consist of a single model. A coding assistant delegates to sub-agents for retrieval, editing, and verification; research prototypes organize dozens of agents into societies that negotiate and trade; and most real systems keep humans in the loop. How well these pieces work together decides how well the system works.

Our evaluation tools have not kept up. Standard benchmarks score one model on one task and say little about coordination overhead, specialization, communication efficiency, or whether adding another agent helps at all. Failure analyses of multi-agent systems point the same way: recurring problems in specification, coordination, and verification are not surfaced by task-level scores.

The first two WMAC editions asked *how to make LLM-based agents collaborate*. In their discussion sessions, one question kept coming back and stayed open: **how do we know a group of agents is actually working well together?** This edition takes that question as its topic.

We aim to make progress on three questions:

<div className={styles.questions}>

1. **What** is worth measuring in a multi-agent or sub-agent system, beyond end-task success?
2. **How** can it be measured reproducibly and comparably across systems?
3. **When** do collectives outperform individuals, and when do they merely add cost?

</div>

## Topics of Interest

Submissions on the following topics are welcome, but not limited to them:

<div className={styles.topics}>

- **Metrics and evaluation methodologies** New metrics for coordination quality, communication efficiency, specialization, and robustness in agent teams. Extending single-agent frameworks such as AgentBoard, and multi-agent arenas such as LLMArena, to measure collective behavior.
- **Benchmarks, environments, and simulations** Controlled environments where collective behavior can be studied reproducibly, including negotiation and social settings, and benchmarks that score collaboration and competition directly.
- **Foundations and theories** Formal definitions and scaling laws for collective intelligence, with connections to how it has been studied in biological and economic systems.
- **Hierarchical and sub-agent architectures** Measurement questions specific to planner–executor and manager–worker structures, where performance is attributable to no single component.
- **Human–agent collectives** Evaluation of mixed human–AI teams and collective decision making.
- **Applications** Case studies from software engineering, scientific discovery, robotics, healthcare, and economics where collective performance was measured, successfully or not.

</div>

## Submission Guidelines

<Tracks />

- **Non-archival:** Presenting here does not prevent publishing elsewhere.
- **Double-blind review:** Submissions will be reviewed on OpenReview, with three reviews per paper.
- **Best Student Paper Award:** For master's and PhD students.
- **Presentations:** We expect 40–50 submissions and roughly 15–20 accepted papers. Three papers will be presented as oral talks; the rest as posters, each with a short lightning pitch.
- **Industry demos:** Demos are welcome. Presenters should prepare a recorded fallback.

**OpenReview submission site: coming soon.**

## Program Format

The full-day program will feature invited talks, oral presentations, lightning talks, a poster session, live industry demos, and a panel discussion. New this year, breakout discussions will bring small groups together around posted questions about what to measure, how to compare systems, and how to evaluate human–agent teams. Each group will report back.
